UX case study: an accessible health claim flow

Student concept project. The company, users and data are fictional; the research plan below describes how the design would be tested with real people.

1. The problem

Submitting a health or dental claim online is often a single long form. Common problems in claim and insurance forms are well known: unclear field formats, errors shown only in red, errors that appear only after submitting, and no way to check answers before sending. These problems hit hardest for people using screen readers, keyboards or small phones.

Goal: a claim flow that anyone can finish in a few minutes, built only from reusable, accessible design system components.

2. Who it is for (assumptions to test)

Priya, 34

Submits dental claims for her family on her phone between tasks. Needs to stop and continue later.

Daniel, 61

Uses a screen reader. Needs clear labels, errors that are announced, and predictable focus.

Mei, 27

Uses keyboard only after a wrist injury. Needs every action reachable with Tab, Enter and arrows.

These are proto-personas: assumptions written down so they can be checked in research, not findings.

High fidelity: the clickable prototype is built entirely from the design system components.

5. Key design decisions

  • One topic per step. Three short steps instead of one long form, with a stepper so users always know where they are.
  • Validate each step, not at the end. Errors appear in text next to the field and in a summary at the top that receives focus and links to each field.
  • Move focus to each new step's heading so screen reader users hear where they are.
  • Formats before input. Hints like “8 digits” and “PDF, JPG or PNG, up to 10 MB” prevent errors instead of reporting them.
  • Review before submit. A “Check your answers” step reduces wrong submissions.
  • Reuse, don't invent. No one-off styles: every element is a design system component, so the next team gets the same accessibility for free.

7. Usability test plan

Who: 5 participants, including at least one keyboard-only user and one screen reader user. How: 20-minute moderated sessions, think-aloud, on the prototype.

  1. “You had a dental cleaning on 3 November. Submit a claim for it.”
  2. “You typed your policy number wrong. Fix it and continue.”
  3. “Before sending, change the claim type to Vision.”

Measures: task success, time on task, number of errors, and a single ease question (1–7) after each task.
